
In this chapter, we present the detailed methodology of the proposed continuous-latent encoder (CLE) for Bengali speech representation. The primary goal is to learn compact continuous representations of Bengali speech that can be effectively utilized for downstream tasks without requiring task-specific encoder adaptation. The proposed model, CLE, produces a 256-dimensional feature representation at 12.5 frames per second. It comprises a 15.29-million parameter frontend and encoder. 

The model is trained on approximately 2,000 hours of speech data, combining waveform reconstruction and similarity loss between embeddings of different views of the same data as the training objective. Furthermore, the model is regularized by a non-heuristic regularizer function known as Variance-Invariance-Sketching Regularization (VISReg), which constrains the embeddings to an isotropic Gaussian distribution. The following sections detail the architecture, representation objectives, VISReg adaptation, auxiliary reconstruction, manifold-constrained hyper-connections (mHC) adaptation, and the data and training configuration.

\section{Architecture}
The architecture of the CLE model is thoughtfully designed to efficiently process speech waveforms and extract meaningful continuous latent representations. As summarized in the conceptual architecture, for a 16 kHz mono waveform $x$, the model employs a convolutional frontend. This frontend consists of five 1D convolution (Conv1D) stages with channel dimensions of [128, 256, 384, 512, 512], kernel sizes of [10, 8, 8, 4, 4], and strides of [5, 4, 4, 4, 4]. Each stage incorporates Group Normalization (GroupNorm) and Gaussian Error Linear Unit (GELU) activation functions. The cumulative stride of the frontend is 1,280 samples, which corresponds to a frame rate of approximately 12.5 frames per second (frames/s).

Following the frontend, an eight-layer, 256-dimensional Conformer-derived encoder is utilized. This encoder features eight attention heads and a 1,024-dimensional feed-forward sublayer. It incorporates rotary full self-attention, macaron feed-forward modules, a 9-tap convolution branch, a dropout rate of 0.1, and squeeze-and-excitation mechanisms. The encoder produces the latent representation $z$:
\begin{equation}
z = E(F(x)), \quad z \in \mathbb{R}^{T' \times 256}
\end{equation}
where $E$ denotes the encoder, $F$ denotes the frontend, and $T'$ is the number of frames. 

To facilitate the similarity loss and VISReg during training, a per-frame Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) projector is applied. This projector maps the 256-dimensional encoder output to a 64-dimensional space through a hidden layer of 512 dimensions ($256 \rightarrow 512 \rightarrow 64$), producing $p = P(z)$. This projection is exclusively used during the training phase. The decoder and small downstream probes directly utilize the encoder embeddings $z$.

The decoder component is responsible for reconstructing the waveform from the latent embeddings. It employs a Feature-wise Linear Modulation (FiLM)-conditioned waveform decoder starting with 768 channels. The decoder effectively reverses the frontend operations with strides of [4, 4, 4, 4, 5] and incorporates two residual blocks per upsampling stage with dilations of [1, 3, 9]. The entire training model contains 23,803,125 parameters, with the frontend, encoder, projector, and decoder containing 2,890,240, 12,403,220, 165,440, and 8,344,225 parameters, respectively. Consequently, the frozen frontend-encoder feature extractor comprises 15,293,460 parameters.

\section{Waveform Views and Similarity Loss}
The training process involves sampling a three-second source segment and generating six augmented views. These consist of two global views and four local views. The two global views are subjected to independently sampled waveform augmentations, representing milder augmentations. The four local views maintain the full three-second duration but undergo more rigorous augmentations, including the masking of consecutive waveform spans. Furthermore, the frontend features for the local views receive an independent frame mask. The waveform augmentations applied include gain changes, additive noise, centered low-pass filtering, and clipping, all designed to preserve the temporal alignment of the signal.

Each waveform mask covers spans in multiples of 1,280 samples, ensuring exact alignment with the frontend's frame spacing. The frontend mask independently hides frames on the same time grid. As a result, a given frame index $t$ corresponds to the same time interval across all views, regardless of whether the corresponding audio or features have been augmented or masked. All six views are processed through the same shared frontend, encoder, and projector.

Let $p_g$ and $p_l$ denote the projector embeddings for the global and local views, respectively. For each training example and frame, $\bar{p}_g$ is defined as the mean of the two global embeddings. The similarity loss is formulated to minimize the distance between the embeddings of the different views:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{view}} = \text{mean}\left[(p_g - \bar{p}_g)^2\right] + \text{mean}\left[(p_l - \bar{p}_g)^2\right]
\end{equation}
By averaging within each group, the global and local views are assigned equal total weight, despite the difference in their counts. This loss function encourages the model to produce similar embeddings for the same frame across different augmented views. It exclusively uses the projector embeddings $p$ without relying on utterance identities or class labels, allowing downstream tasks to be evaluated separately using the frozen encoder features $z$.

\section{VISReg Adaptation}
Variance-Invariance-Sketching Regularization (VISReg) is employed to regularize the frame-level embeddings, guiding them toward an isotropic Gaussian distribution to discourage representational collapse. The projector embeddings are collected into a set of $B = T'VB_{\text{global}}$ points in $D = 64$ dimensions, where $V = 6$ represents the number of views and $B_{\text{global}}$ is the source-segment batch size across workers. Frames are gathered across workers without averaging over time.

For embeddings $q_i \in \mathbb{R}^D$, VISReg penalizes a nonzero mean, subtracts the mean, computes a per-dimension Root Mean Square (RMS) scale with division by $\sqrt{B}$, and penalizes scales that deviate from one. The embeddings are normalized by this scale without passing gradients through the scale parameter. During every forward pass, 256 normalized Gaussian directions are sampled. The values projected onto each direction are sorted and matched to standard-normal quantiles $\Phi^{-1}(i/(B+1))$. The regularization terms for the mean, scale, and distribution shape are assigned equal weight. 

This formulation adapts VISReg’s mean, scale, and distribution-shape regularization to the CLE’s frame-level embeddings and distributed training setup. The adaptation gathers frames across workers, adds a small constant ($10^{-6}$) to the scale to ensure numerical stability, assigns equal weights to the three components, and utilizes the same data type for target quantiles and activations, with training executed in BF16 precision.

\section{Reconstruction and Total Objective}
The auxiliary decoder receives the embeddings $z$ from the first global view. Prior to decoding, an independent span mask removes 50\% of the frames in spans of 4 to 24 frames, and Gaussian noise with a standard deviation of 0.03 is added to the latent representation. The reconstruction target remains the original clean source waveform. 

The decoder generates a waveform, which is then compared against the clean waveform using an 80-bin log-mel loss. This loss is computed with a Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) size of 1,024, a window length of 1,024, a hop length of 256, a Hann window, a magnitude weight of 1.0, a log-magnitude weight of 1.0, and a spectral-convergence weight of 0. Multi-resolution Short-Time Fourier Transform (STFT) is reserved exclusively for evaluation purposes.

The total training objective combines the mel reconstruction loss, the view similarity loss, and the VISReg regularization loss:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{total}} = 1.0\mathcal{L}_{\text{mel}} + 0.3\mathcal{L}_{\text{view}} + 0.7\mathcal{L}_{\text{VISReg}}
\end{equation}
These three coefficients are carefully chosen to weight the losses according to their different numerical scales. It is important to note that the training process does not involve any quantization, variational bottlenecks, or adversarial objectives, distinguishing it from discrete codec-based approaches.

\section{mHC Adaptation}
Manifold-Constrained Hyper-Connections (mHC) are integrated into the architecture to enhance the residual pathways. Specifically, at the third and sixth encoder layers, the mHC mechanism expands the residual path into two distinct streams. It employs a learned static mixing matrix before and after each complete encoder layer. 

The residual mixing matrix is mathematically constrained using ten Sinkhorn iterations and subsequently blended with the identity matrix to maintain stability. The CLE model then averages these two streams to return to a single unified pathway. This approach provides a compact adaptation of mHC, the effects of which have been empirically observed to vary across different downstream probing tasks, thereby contributing to the model's representational flexibility.

It is important to note that while Common Voice and OpenSLR-53 contribute to the pretraining phase, evaluations conducted on these sources are considered in-domain frozen-feature evaluations. The training inventory does not strictly enforce demographic or dialect balance, and the SUBESCO dataset, which is utilized for downstream emotion evaluation, is explicitly excluded from the pretraining inventory to ensure a fair assessment.

The model is trained for 210,000 steps using the AdamW optimizer with a batch size of 42 and gradient accumulation over four batches on a single worker, resulting in an effective batch size of 168 source segments. With each segment producing six augmented views, the training process is computationally rigorous. The learning rate schedule incorporates a base learning rate reduced to $5 \times 10^{-4}$ at step 50,000, with subsequent continuations using $10^{-4}$ alongside a 300,000-step scheduler horizon. Gradient clipping is applied at a threshold of 1.0, and the training is executed using BF16 precision to optimize memory and computational efficiency.
