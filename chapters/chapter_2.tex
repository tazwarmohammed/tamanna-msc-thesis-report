Several related studies have explored self-supervised learning (SSL) for speech representation, leading to significant advancements in automatic speech recognition (ASR) and other downstream tasks. This section summarizes these studies, focusing on the advancement of SSL techniques, the evolution of loss functions, foundation models for low-resource languages, and their specific application to Bengali speech.

The transition from supervised deep learning to self-supervised paradigms has fundamentally reshaped speech representation learning. As highlighted by Borgholt et al. \cite{borgholt2022brief}, SSL methods effectively mitigate the dependency on large, explicitly transcribed datasets by leveraging vast amounts of unlabeled data. This shift has made it increasingly viable to develop universal models that generalize across various acoustic domains, dialects, and low-resource scenarios, utilizing generative, contrastive, and predictive modeling approaches.

Central to the success of these SSL foundation models is the evolution of their underlying loss functions. Early approaches often relied on simple waveform or spectrogram reconstruction objectives (such as L1 or L2 loss). However, modern frameworks have introduced more sophisticated techniques. For instance, Cho et al. \cite{cho2022noncontrastive} demonstrated the efficacy of non-contrastive self-supervised learning for utterance-level information extraction, bypassing the need for complex negative sampling while achieving superior performance in downstream tasks. In the domain of speech enhancement, Close et al. \cite{close2023perceive} proposed using the perceptual distance between clean and noisy representations in self-supervised feature spaces directly as a loss function. This representation-based loss closely correlates with human intelligibility ratings and significantly outperforms traditional time-domain or signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) based loss functions. Furthermore, Sivaraman et al. \cite{sivaraman2021efficient} explored self-supervised data purification and contrastive learning regularizations for personalized, efficient speech enhancement, proving that tailored SSL objectives can greatly reduce computational complexity while maintaining high fidelity.

These advancements in self-supervised architectures and loss formulations have birthed massive foundation models. Hsu et al. \cite{hsu2021hubert} introduced HuBERT, which utilizes a masked prediction loss over hidden units. By clustering continuous inputs offline, HuBERT learns a combined acoustic and language model, achieving up to a 19\% relative word error rate (WER) reduction compared to earlier contrastive models like wav2vec 2.0. Similarly, Chen et al. \cite{chen2021wavlm} proposed WavLM, which jointly learns masked speech prediction and denoising objectives, establishing state-of-the-art robustness on the SUPERB benchmark.

While models like HuBERT and WavLM represent the cutting edge for high-resource languages, adapting them to low-resource languages remains challenging. Recent works have heavily focused on creating large-scale speech foundation models specifically for low-resource minority languages \cite{large_scale_minority} and exploring depth-aware adaptation strategies for efficient multilingual recognition \cite{adapting_where_it_matters}. These studies emphasize that while cross-lingual transfer from high-resource foundation models is beneficial, there is a critical need for efficient adaptation techniques that account for the unique phonetic characteristics and limited data availability of regional dialects. 

Pushing the boundaries of multilingual pre-training, Babu et al. \cite{babu2021xlsr} introduced XLS-R, a massive cross-lingual speech representation model based on wav2vec 2.0 with up to 2 billion parameters. By training on nearly half a million hours of speech across 128 languages, XLS-R demonstrated that massive cross-lingual pre-training can significantly lower error rates across diverse low-resource languages. Building on this, Ogunremi et al. \cite{ogunremi2023multilingual} showed that fine-tuning wav2vec 2.0 XLSR for low-resource African languages with code-switching substantially outperforms traditional hybrid acoustic models trained from scratch on limited data. In the context of Indic languages, Holla et al. \cite{holla2022end} fine-tuned a large-scale self-supervised model for Sanskrit ASR, achieving state-of-the-art results compared to older methods. Similarly, Vaddepalli et al. \cite{vaddepalli2026multilingual} fine-tuned the Wav2Vec2-Large-XLSR-53 model with statistical language modeling to improve Telugu speech recognition, emphasizing the necessity of robust cross-lingual representations for agglutinative and phonetically complex languages.

However, leveraging these massive models introduces severe computational bottlenecks, especially for low-resource deployment. To mitigate the prohibitive expense of full model fine-tuning, Chen et al. \cite{chen2024meta} developed a Meta-Adapter structure that enables quick and efficient parameter adaptation for low-resource languages without updating the entire foundation model. Addressing the computational cost of the pre-training phase itself, Zhang et al. \cite{zhang2023fast} proposed an unsupervised data selection method based on language similarity, saving 60\% of training steps while maintaining high performance. Furthermore, Lin et al. \cite{lin2022smaller} comprehensively analyzed the tradeoffs in compressing self-supervised speech Transformers through techniques like pruning and knowledge distillation, highlighting the ongoing struggle to balance model size, real-time inference speed, and accuracy in practical scenarios.

Beyond standard speech recognition, self-supervised representations have proven highly effective for generating specialized embeddings in complex downstream applications. Fujita et al. \cite{fujita2023zero} proposed a zero-shot text-to-speech synthesis framework conditioned entirely on self-supervised speech representations, highlighting how disentangling rhythmic and acoustic features in the SSL latent space can dramatically improve the reproduction of unseen speaker characteristics. In the realm of demographic specialization, Lu et al. \cite{lu2022improving} demonstrated that fine-tuning adult self-supervised speech representations on as little as 5 hours of transcribed children’s speech can yield a recognition system that outperforms state-of-the-art models trained on 960 hours of adult speech, underlining the remarkable transferability and generalization of robust SSL embeddings across distinct acoustic domains.

Focusing specifically on the Bengali language, researchers have predominantly investigated transfer learning from these massive multilingual models. Samin et al. \cite{samin2024banspeech} evaluated self-supervised wav2vec 2.0 against weakly supervised Whisper models on the multi-domain BanSpeech benchmark, concluding that cross-lingual pre-training is highly effective, though vulnerable to spontaneous speech degradation. Hossain et al. \cite{hossain2024enhancing} fine-tuned wav2vec 2.0 on OpenSLR and self-compiled datasets, achieving impressive WER (11.27\%) and Character Error Rate (CER) (6.03\%) for local speech-to-text conversion. Addressing environmental noise and dialectal diversity, Biswas et al. \cite{biswas2025unified} proposed a multi-stage fine-tuning strategy for WavLM, significantly outperforming standard fine-tuned models under low SNR conditions.

Despite these successes, the predominant reliance on fine-tuning massive models (ranging from hundreds of millions to billions of parameters) imposes significant computational overhead. These models are not inherently optimized from the ground up for the specific phonological intricacies of Bengali. This underscores a prominent research gap: the development of a highly compact, dedicated foundation model trained entirely from scratch on Bengali datasets using advanced SSL objectives.
